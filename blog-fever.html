Child Fever: When to Worry and When to Watch

A fever at midnight frightens every parent who has ever held a hot, restless little one. Most fevers are the body doing its job well. A few need a doctor immediately. Here is how to tell them apart, calmly.

First, What Fever Actually Is

Fever is not the enemy. It is the body raising its own temperature to fight an infection, and in most children it does this efficiently and safely. A normal body temperature sits around 37 degrees Celsius. Generally, 38 degrees and above counts as a fever in a child.

How hot the child feels to your hand is not a reliable measure. Use a thermometer. If you do not have one, that alone is a good reason to visit a pharmacy today, before you ever need it at 2 am.

See a Doctor Immediately If

  • The baby is under three months old with any fever of 38 degrees or more. This is always an emergency, day or night.
  • A seizure or convulsion occurs, with shaking movements and eyes rolling back.
  • A rash appears that does not fade when you press a glass against it.
  • The child is unusually drowsy, difficult to wake, limp, or not responding to you normally.
  • Drinking very little, passing urine far less than usual, or no tears when crying.
  • Trouble breathing, noisy breathing, or the skin between the ribs pulling in with each breath.
  • The fever has lasted more than three days, or keeps returning after the child seemed to recover.
In babies under three months, fever is never "just a fever". Our emergency department is open at every hour, and a feverish infant will always be seen quickly.

When You Can Watch at Home

If your child is over three months, drinking fluids reasonably well, still playing or at least interested in the world between doses of medicine, and has no signs from the list above, you can usually care for the fever at home for a day or two.

Dress the child lightly, offer fluids often, keep the room comfortably cool, and use paracetamol at the dose written on the pack for the child's age and weight. Never give aspirin to a child. Do not sponge with cold water or alcohol, as these old methods do more harm than good.

Trust the Behavior More Than the Number

Here is the quiet truth experienced pediatricians repeat to each other: a child at 39 degrees who is drinking, complaining and asking to watch cartoons is usually in better shape than a child at 38 degrees who is silent and still. The number on the thermometer tells you part of the story. How your child is behaving tells you more.
